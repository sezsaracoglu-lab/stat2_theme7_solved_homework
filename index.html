The rules of the game

Ten lines. Everything else in this page follows from them, so if you learn nothing else before the exam, learn these.

  1. Two factors, one score each. Two categorical variables sort the same people at the same time, and every person gives exactly one number. That is the whole design.
  2. Levels × levels = cells. The name of the design IS the multiplication. A 3 × 2 has 6 cells; a 3 × 4 has 12. Count the cells before you do anything else — almost every df needs that number.
  3. Three questions, three F values. Main effect of A, main effect of B, interaction A × B. One analysis, one table, three separate verdicts.
  4. Main effects live in the MARGINS; the interaction lives in the CELLS. A main effect compares row means or column means. The interaction is about the pattern of the four (or six, or twelve) cell means.
  5. All the spread splits, and then splits again. SS Total = SS Between + SS Within(Error). And SS Between = SS(A) + SS(B) + SS(A × B). SS Within never splits.
  6. Every df is “minus one” — except the interaction, which MULTIPLIES. df(A) = levels of A − 1. df(B) = levels of B − 1. df(A × B) = df(A) × df(B). df Error = N − cells. df Total = N − 1.
  7. Divide ACROSS the row, compare DOWN the column. MS = SS ÷ df on the same row, never across rows. Then every F = that MS ÷ the ONE MS Error.
  8. An F is meaningless without its pair of df. Always F(effect df, error df) — the effect’s own df first, the error df second. The two orders have completely different critical values.
  9. Parallel means no interaction. In a plot of cell means: parallel lines → no interaction; non-parallel (including crossing) → interaction. Crossing is just the extreme case.
  10. Read the interaction FIRST. If it is significant, the main effects can no longer be read at face value and you must describe the cell means. This is the lecture’s own caution, and it is where exam marks hide.

The method: symptom → diagnosis → treatment

When a question stops you, it is almost never the arithmetic. It is that you are reading the words and hunting for a formula at the same time, and those two jobs fight each other.

A doctor never does that. Nobody looks at a patient and reaches straight for a prescription: they take the symptoms, they name the diagnosis, and only then does the treatment follow — and by then the treatment is not a decision any more. It is already written down.

Two-factor ANOVA has exactly seven diagnoses. All seventeen questions in this homework are one of them, and each one has a fixed procedure and sits on a known step of the six-step machine. So the whole skill is step ②, and it takes about five seconds.

The test of whether you have really diagnosed

You should be able to say the case type out loud BEFORE you look at the answer options at all. If you cannot, do not look at them yet — a well-written wrong option is designed to look right to someone who skipped step ②.

1Name the parts

stands on step 1 · Design
① Symptom — what the words look like
  • “The two factors being studied are…”
  • “The response variable is…” · “the dependent variable is…”
  • “How many levels does each factor have?”
③ Treatment — every time
  1. Find the MEASURED NUMBER first. That is the response (dependent) variable, always quantitative, one per person.
  2. Everything else that sorts people into groups is a factor. There are exactly two.
  3. The categories of each factor are its levels. Count them: those two counts are a and b.
  4. The levels are NOT factors, and a combination of levels is NOT a factor — it is a cell.
Differential — the type this gets confused with
Case 2 — count the design. Case 1 asks what the variables ARE; case 2 turns those names into numbers.
⚠ The mis-diagnosis
Offering the levels of one factor as “the two factors” (men and women), or offering the response variable as a factor (heart rate and smoking level). The response is the thing measured, never a grouping.

2Count the design — cells, N and every df

stands on step 1 · Design → 3 · Average
① Symptom — what the words look like
  • “The degrees of freedom for interaction are…”
  • “The degrees of freedom for error are…”
  • “How many subjects in total?” · “the test statistic is an F with … df”
③ Treatment — every time
  1. Write a and b: the number of levels of each factor.
  2. cells = a × b. If each cell holds n people, N = n × a × b.
  3. df(A) = a − 1. df(B) = b − 1. df(A × B) = df(A) × df(B) — multiply, never add.
  4. df Error = N − cells. df Total = N − 1.
  5. Check: df(A) + df(B) + df(A×B) + df Error = N − 1. If it does not, one of them is wrong.
Differential — the type this gets confused with
Case 3 — fill the table. Case 2 asks for a df as the ANSWER; in case 3 a df is only a step on the way to an MS or an F.
⚠ The mis-diagnosis
Adding the two df instead of multiplying them for the interaction, and using N − 1 or N − 2 for the error df instead of N − cells. In a 2 × 2 the two mistakes are invisible, because 1 × 1 = 1 + 1 − 1.

3Fill the table — MS and F

stands on step 3 · Average → 4 · Ratio
① Symptom — what the words look like
  • “The value of MS-something is…”
  • “The F statistic for … is…”
  • any printed ANOVA table with blanks in it
③ Treatment — every time
  1. MS = SS ÷ df, on the SAME row. Never across rows.
  2. Find MS Error first: SS Error ÷ df Error. It is the denominator of every F in the table.
  3. F(effect) = MS(effect) ÷ MS Error.
  4. Backwards is the same rules rearranged: SS = MS × df, and N = df Error + cells.
  5. SS and df both ADD down the column, so a missing Total or a missing row is one subtraction away.
Differential — the type this gets confused with
Case 2 — degrees of freedom. A table is full of df, so case-2 instincts fire; but here the df is bookkeeping, not the answer.
⚠ The mis-diagnosis
Dividing SS by SS to get F. F is a ratio of MEAN squares, so both have to be converted first — and that conversion is what the df are for.

4How the table is built

stands on step 2 · Split → 4 · Ratio
① Symptom — what the words look like
  • “Which of the following statements about the analysis of variance is/are TRUE?”
  • “The total sum of squares is composed of…”
  • “The test statistic is the ratio of…”
③ Treatment — every time
  1. SS Total = SS for the effects + SS Error. Sums of squares add; mean squares do not.
  2. The test statistic is a ratio of MEAN squares, never of raw sums of squares.
  3. Every sum of squares is a sum of SQUARED deviations — that is where the name comes from.
  4. In a one-way table the rows are groups, error and total; in a two-way table the “groups” row has been split into A, B and A × B.
Differential — the type this gets confused with
Case 3 — fill the table. Case 4 asks about the table’s STRUCTURE in words; case 3 asks you to compute a cell of it.
⚠ The mis-diagnosis
Accepting “the ratio of SSG to SSE” because it looks like the F formula. It is the ratio of MSG to MSE. One word, the whole mark.

5Read the plot

stands on step 6 · Follow up
① Symptom — what the words look like
  • “This plot indicates / suggests that…”
  • any interaction plot or means plot with no numbers attached
  • “Are the lines parallel?”
③ Treatment — every time
  1. Look at PARALLEL first. Parallel → no interaction. Not parallel, or crossing → interaction.
  2. Then look at SLOPE. If the lines, on average, slope rather than run flat, the factor on the x-axis is doing something.
  3. Then look at SEPARATION. A vertical gap between the lines means the factor drawn as separate lines is doing something.
  4. Say only what the picture SUGGESTS. A plot never establishes significance — only an F test does.
Differential — the type this gets confused with
Case 6 — interpret the output. Case 5 works from a picture with no numbers; case 6 works from a table with p-values.
⚠ The mis-diagnosis
Calling a small wobble an interaction. Real lines are almost never exactly parallel; the question is whether they clearly fan out or cross.

6Interpret the output

stands on step 5 · Decide → 6 · Follow up
① Symptom — what the words look like
  • “We can conclude from the ANOVA table that…”
  • “If we reject the null hypothesis we conclude that…”
  • “What does p = 0.000 mean here?”
③ Treatment — every time
  1. Go effect by effect. Each row has its own p, and each p answers its own question.
  2. A non-significant interaction licenses one specific sentence: the effect of one factor appears to be the SAME at every level of the other.
  3. A significant main effect says the means are NOT all equal. It does NOT say which, in what order, or in which direction.
  4. Keep the conclusion about the POPULATION and keep it modest — “at least one differs”, never “they all differ”.
Differential — the type this gets confused with
Case 5 — read the plot. Both are interpretation; case 6 has p-values in front of it and must therefore be precise about which effect each claim belongs to.
⚠ The mis-diagnosis
Upgrading a significant F into a direction. “Heart rate INCREASES with amount smoked” is a trend claim; the F test only says the three means are not all equal.

7Design logic — why two factors, and what to do next

stands on step 0 · Design choice → 6 · Follow up
① Symptom — what the words look like
  • “A major benefit of two-way ANOVA over several one-way ANOVAs is…”
  • “If an F test for interaction is significant…”
  • “Why not just run two separate analyses?”
③ Treatment — every time
  1. The advantages: more efficient; smaller samples per condition; less residual variation; and the interaction becomes testable.
  2. Only the last of those is impossible any other way — a series of one-way ANOVAs can never test an interaction.
  3. If the interaction IS significant: plot the means, and examine the CELL means carefully, because the marginal means no longer tell the whole story.
  4. The main effects do not become forbidden — they become insufficient on their own.
Differential — the type this gets confused with
Case 6 — interpret the output. Case 7 is about the DESIGN and what to do next; case 6 is about what a particular set of numbers means.
⚠ The mis-diagnosis
Answering “you always then test the two single factors” when an interaction is significant. The required next step is examining the means, not another test.

Key terms — read these first

FactorA categorical variable that sorts people into groups. A two-factor design has exactly two; this homework calls them things like diet, gender, oven, field.
LevelOne category of one factor. “Low fat” is a level of diet. How many levels each factor has is a and b.
Cell / treatment combinationOne level of A paired with one level of B. The number of cells is a × b, and it is the number every error df subtracts.
Response variableThe quantitative thing measured on each person — weight loss, yield, heart rate, lifetime in minutes. Also called the dependent variable.
Main effectThe effect of one factor on its own, with the other averaged away. It compares marginal (row or column) means.
InteractionThe effect of one factor is different at different levels of the other. In a plot: non-parallel lines.
Marginal meanA row mean or a column mean — one factor collapsed over the other. SPSS calls these “estimated marginal means”, which is the label on the plot in question 5.
SS (sum of squares)A sum of squared deviations. SS Total = SS for the effects + SS Error, always, with no remainder.
MS (mean square)A sum of squares divided by its OWN degrees of freedom. This is what makes two sources comparable.
MS ErrorSS Error ÷ df Error. The one noise estimate, and the denominator of all three F values. Sometimes printed as MS Within or MSE.
F statisticMS for an effect ÷ MS Error. Around 1 when that effect’s H₀ is true; well above 1 when it is not.
df interaction(a − 1)(b − 1). The only degrees of freedom in the whole theme that are a product rather than a subtraction.
df errorN − (a × b) — the total number of scores minus the number of cells. It is the second number in every F you report.
p-valueThe probability of a statistic this extreme IF that row’s H₀ were true. Small p → reject that H₀. It belongs to its own row and nowhere else.
Partial R²SS(effect) ÷ [SS(effect) + SS Error]. The effect size this course reports for a two-factor ANOVA.
Response vs factorThe single most useful habit: find the measured NUMBER first. That is the response. Everything that groups people is a factor.

Learning objectives

  • Name the two factors, their levels and the response variable from a description.
  • Count the cells and produce every degree of freedom from a and b and N — the interaction df included.
  • Fill any gap in a two-factor ANOVA table, forwards or backwards.
  • Compute each F against the single shared MS Error, and report it with the right pair of df.
  • Read an interaction plot: parallel or not, sloping, separated.
  • Say what a significant interaction obliges you to do next, and what it does to the main effects.
  • Turn a p-value into a decision about H₀ and a properly modest sentence about the population.
  • Explain why one two-way ANOVA beats two one-way ANOVAs — and name the thing only it can test.

Two factors are described in words and you are asked for ONE number: the interaction degrees of freedom. Notice what the question does NOT give you — any sample size. That absence is a clue, not an omission: the interaction df cannot need it.

›
  • a.two-way is more efficient and gives more information.
  • b.two-way takes less time.
  • c.two one-ways are better than one two-way.
① Symptom — what the question is really asking
No arithmetic at all. The question is about WHY this design exists — what you gain by crossing two factors in one study instead of analysing them separately.
② Diagnosis
Case type 7 · Design logic — why two factors, and what to do next
which puts you on step 0 · Design choice of the machine
③ Treatment — the procedure
  1. List what the lecture says you gain. More efficient: two research questions answered by one study and one sample. Smaller samples needed per condition, because everyone at every level of B also contributes to the comparison for A.
  2. Less residual variation: in a one-way ANOVA on A, any effect of B is swept into the error term and inflates it. Pulling B out gives it its own row and shrinks MS Error.
  3. And the decisive one: interactions become testable — and they CANNOT be tested with a series of one-way ANOVAs.
  4. Now read the options and find the one that covers that list. “More efficient and gives more information” is exactly it: efficiency is the first item, and “more information” is the interaction plus the cleaner error term.

✓a. Correct. Efficiency plus extra information — above all the interaction, which separate one-way analyses can never reach.
✗b. Not the statistical benefit, and not reliably true either: the analysis is one procedure rather than two, but running the study takes however long it takes. Exams reward the statistical reason, not the convenience one.
✗c. The opposite of the lecture’s position. Two one-ways lose the interaction entirely and leave each other’s variance in the error term.
Carry this forward
If an option mentions time, convenience or effort, it is almost certainly not the answer to a “why this design?” question. Look for information and precision instead.

›

① Symptom — what the question is really asking
You have one result — a significant interaction — and the question asks what that obliges you to do next. This is the lecture’s caution slide, turned into a question.
② Diagnosis

which puts you on step 6 · Follow up of the machine
③ Treatment — the procedure
  1. Remember what a significant interaction means: the effect of one factor is DIFFERENT at different levels of the other.
  2. That immediately damages the marginal means. A row mean averages over the levels of the other factor, so when the effect changes across those levels, the average describes nobody in particular.
  3. The lecture’s instruction is explicit: when an interaction is present, examine the CELL means, because the marginal means do not tell the whole story.

✗b. Not false, but weak: “there MAY be interest” describes an option, not a consequence. The question asks what follows from a significant interaction, and what follows is a requirement, not a possibility. If you are torn between this and (c), pick the one that states an obligation.

The interaction plot was obtained. This plot indicates:

A picture, no numbers, and the word “indicates”. You are being asked to read the shape of the lines — nothing more.
② Diagnosis

③ Treatment — the procedure
  1. Identify the axes. Field runs along the bottom (four levels); each line is one variety of alfalfa (six of them); the vertical axis is mean yield.
  2. Ask question ① — are the lines parallel? They are not. They rise and fall differently from field to field, and several of them cross so the order of the varieties changes.
  3. Non-parallel lines mean the effect of field is not the same for every variety — which is the definition of an interaction.
  4. Check the other two options are not better. There is clearly a spread between the lines, so “field only” is wrong. And the lines move as you walk across the fields, so “alfalfa type only” is wrong too. But the question asks what the plot INDICATES, and the striking feature is the crossing.

✗a. That is 96 ÷ 288 — sums of squares divided by sums of squares. F always divides mean squares.
✗c. That is MS lighting itself, copied over with no division. An MS is not an F.
✗d. That is 48 ÷ 18 — divided by the INTERACTION mean square instead of the error mean square.
🏷️ When the F column is blank, the first thing you write is MS Error. It is the denominator of every F in the table.
Question 5 · case type 5 · Read the plot  ·  Match the picture to the words
In a plot of cell means, one line rises steeply from left to right while the other falls by about as much, so the two cross near the middle of the graph. Which description fits best?
  • a.a main effect of the line factor, with no interaction
  • b.two main effects and no interaction between them
  • c.a clear interaction, and perhaps no main effects at all
  • d.no effects of any kind in this study
Crossing lines are non-parallel, so an interaction is present — that part is certain. And because the two slopes are equal and opposite, they cancel when you average them: every column mean comes out about the same, and so does every row mean. That is the classic “interaction with no main effects” picture. (Careful: this only works because the slopes OPPOSE. One flat line crossing one steep line still leaves a main effect of the x-axis factor.)
The other options
✗a. A main effect of the line factor would show as a consistent vertical gap. Here the gap reverses.
✗b. Two main effects with no interaction would be parallel, sloping, separated lines — the opposite of crossing.
✗d. Crossing lines are an effect. “Nothing going on” is two flat lines on top of each other.
🏷️ Crossing is the extreme case of non-parallel, and a SYMMETRIC crossing — equal and opposite slopes — is the one pattern where the interaction is strong and both main effects can vanish entirely.

Question 7 · case type 7 · Design logic — why two factors, and what to do next  ·  What did they lose?
A colleague has data from a 2 × 4 study but runs two separate one-way ANOVAs — one on each factor — instead of a single two-way. What has that cost her, that no amount of care can recover from those two outputs?
  • a.she can no longer compute an effect size for either factor
  • b.she has no test of whether the two factors interact
  • c.her degrees of freedom are wrong in both analyses
  • d.she can no longer say anything about either factor
Two one-way ANOVAs will each give a perfectly valid test of their own factor, with correct degrees of freedom and effect sizes. What they cannot do, in any combination, is test whether the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other. The lecture states this directly: interactions cannot be tested with a series of one-way ANOVAs.
The other options
✗a. Each one-way analysis reports its own R² quite happily.
✗c. The df are correct for the analyses she actually ran — they are just the one-way df, and each error term is larger than it needed to be.
✗d. Both main effects are still testable; they are simply tested less precisely, because the other factor’s variation sits in the error term.
🏷️ Separate analyses lose precision AND the interaction. Only the second of those is impossible to recover.
Question 8 · case type 2 · Count the design — cells, N and every df  ·  Which value is impossible
Factor A has 4 levels. Which of the following CANNOT be the interaction degrees of freedom, whatever the number of levels of factor B?
  • a.3
  • b.6
  • c.8
  • d.9
df A×B = (a − 1)(b − 1) = 3 × (b − 1), so every possible value is a multiple of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12 and so on. 8 is not a multiple of 3, so no number of levels of B can produce it.
The other options
✗a. b = 2 gives 3 × 1 = 3.
✗b. b = 3 gives 3 × 2 = 6.
✗d. b = 4 gives 3 × 3 = 9.
🏷️ Because the interaction df is a PRODUCT, it is always a multiple of each main-effect df. That makes some values impossible — a quick sanity check on your own arithmetic.
Question 9 · case type 3 · Fill the table — MS and F  ·  Recover the sum of squares
A two-factor table reports F = 12.5 for a factor with 3 degrees of freedom, and MS Error = 4.8. What is the sum of squares for that factor?
  • a.180
  • b.60
  • c.37.5
  • d.12.5
Work the chain backwards. F = MS ÷ MS Error, so MS = F × MS Error = 12.5 × 4.8 = 60. Then SS = MS × df = 60 × 3 = 180.
The other options
✗b. That is the mean square — one step short. The question asked for the sum of squares, which still needs multiplying by the df.
✗c. That is 12.5 × 3 — the F multiplied by the df, with MS Error never used.
✗d. That is F itself.
🏷️ The chain is SS → ÷df → MS → ÷MSE → F. To go backwards, reverse it and multiply: F × MSE = MS, then MS × df = SS.
Question 10 · case type 4 · How the table is built  ·  True or false, one at a time
Which statements about a TWO-factor ANOVA table are TRUE?

I. The sums of squares for the two factors, the interaction and the error add to the total sum of squares.

II. Each factor has its own mean square error.

III. The degrees of freedom add in exactly the same way the sums of squares do.

IV. The mean squares add to a total mean square.

  • a.I and II only
  • b.I and III only
  • c.I, III and IV only
  • d.all four
I is true — that is the partition the whole method rests on. III is true — df split exactly as SS do, which is why they are a free check on each other. II is false: there is ONE error term, and all three F values divide by it. IV is false: each mean square has already been divided by a DIFFERENT number of degrees of freedom, so they cannot simply be added — which is also why the table has no MS Total row.
The other options
✗a. Includes II. There is only one MS Error in the table, shared by every effect.
✗c. Includes IV. Sums of squares add; mean squares do not, which is also why the table has no MS Total.
✗d. Includes both false statements.
🏷️ SS adds. df adds. MS does not — it is already divided by something, and you cannot add averages that were taken over different numbers of pieces.
Question 11 · case type 5 · Read the plot  ·  Describe the picture you would expect
A 2 × 2 study finds a strong main effect of factor A, no main effect of factor B, and no interaction. What should the plot of cell means look like, with A on the x-axis?

  • d.two sloping lines almost on top of each other
A on the x-axis and a main effect of A means the lines SLOPE. No main effect of B means the two lines are not separated — they sit on top of each other. No interaction means they are parallel. Sloping, together and parallel.

Question 12 · case type 6 · Interpret the output  ·  What else do you need?
A study reports a significant main effect of practice amount, F(2, 87) = 11.2, p < .001, on a memory test. A student wants to write “memory improves as practice increases.” What does she need before she can write that?
  • a.a larger sample
  • b.the interaction to be non-significant as well
  • c.the three group means, to see which way they go
  • d.nothing — a significant F already means the scores increase
A significant F says the three means are not all equal. It carries no direction and no ordering: the pattern could be high–low–high, or one group far below the other two. To claim an increase you have to look at the means themselves (and, to claim which pairs differ, at post-hoc comparisons).
The other options
✗a. The sample is already large enough to have produced a significant result.
✗b. The interaction is a separate question and does not license a direction either.
✗d. This is exactly the over-claim. “Not all equal” is not “increases”.
🏷️ F tells you THAT the means differ. Only the means tell you HOW. Any sentence with a direction in it needs the means quoted beside it.
Question 13 · case type 1 · Name the parts  ·  Count and name in one go

Question 14 · case type 3 · Fill the table — MS and F  ·  Diagnose someone else’s answer
A student computes F for a factor as 240 ÷ 600 = 0.40 and concludes there is no effect. The table shows SS factor = 240 on 2 df and SS Error = 600 on 40 df. What is the correct F, and what did she do wrong?
  • a.0.40 — her working is right
  • b.8.00 — she divided sums of squares instead of mean squares
  • c.0.40, but the conclusion should have been the opposite
  • d.2.50 — she inverted the ratio
F is a ratio of MEAN squares. MS factor = 240 ÷ 2 = 120; MS Error = 600 ÷ 40 = 15; F = 120 ÷ 15 = 8.00 on F(2, 40) — a large effect, not a missing one. Her mistake was skipping the df entirely, which is precisely what the df are there to prevent.
The other options
✗a. Dividing SS by SS leaves out the whole point of degrees of freedom.
✗c. The number is wrong, so the conclusion drawn from it cannot be rescued.
✗d. Inverting is a different error; here nothing was inverted, the conversion to mean squares was simply never done.
🏷️ An F built straight from sums of squares will almost always come out too small, because SS Error is large and carries many more df. If your F looks suspiciously tiny, check whether you divided by the df.
Question 15 · case type 2 · Count the design — cells, N and every df  ·  Read the design out of the F
A one-way ANOVA on a comfort rating is reported as F(4, 40) = 3.1. How many groups were compared, and how many people took part?
  • a.5 groups, 45 people
  • b.4 groups, 40 people
  • c.5 groups, 44 people
  • d.4 groups, 45 people
In a one-way ANOVA the df are (k − 1, N − k). So k − 1 = 4 gives k = 5, and N − k = 40 gives N = 40 + 5 = 45. Check: 4 + 40 = 44 = N − 1 ✓
The other options
✗b. Reads the two df as the counts themselves, with no subtraction undone.
✗c. Right number of groups, but N has been taken as df Total + 0. N = df Within + k, and that is 45.
✗d. Right N, wrong k — the numerator df is k − 1, so k is one MORE than 4.
🏷️ Every df rule runs in both directions. A printed F(a, b) for a one-way hands you k = a + 1 and N = b + k.
Question 16 · case type 7 · Design logic — why two factors, and what to do next  ·  Find the odd one out
Which of these is NOT an advantage of a two-factor design and its single two-way ANOVA, compared with studying the two factors in separate one-way designs?
  • a.the interaction between the factors becomes testable
  • b.the error term is smaller, because the second factor is taken out of it
  • c.fewer participants are needed per condition
  • d.the risk of a false positive is lower, because only one analysis is run
The first three are the lecture’s own advantages. The fourth is not: a two-factor ANOVA runs THREE tests, not one, so it carries at least as much false-positive risk as two one-way analyses would. The gains are in information and precision, not in error rate.
The other options
✗a. A genuine advantage — and the only one that is impossible any other way.
✗b. A genuine advantage: pulling the second factor out of the error term shrinks MS Error and raises every F.
✗c. A genuine advantage: everyone at every level of B also contributes to the comparison for A.
🏷️ Two-factor designs buy information and precision. They do not buy a lower false-positive rate — three tests are three tests.
Question 17 · case type 6 · Interpret the output  ·  Mark someone else’s sentence
A student submits this sentence: “All four feedback styles differed in their effect on motivation, F(3, 76) = 4.9, p = .004.” The statistics are reported correctly. What has to change for the sentence to be right?
  • a.“all four … differed” must become “at least one differed from the others”
  • b.the p value must be reported as p < .05 rather than p = .004
  • c.the degrees of freedom should be written F(4, 76), one per group
  • d.nothing — with p = .004 the sentence is already correct
The F test is sound and the reporting is fine: four groups give 3 numerator df, and p = .004 is a legitimate exact p. The fault is the claim. Rejecting H₀ (“all four means are equal”) leaves you with “not all equal” — at least one differs. Saying all four differ from each other needs post-hoc comparisons the student has not run.
The other options
✗b. An exact p is perfectly acceptable and more informative than an inequality.
✗c. Numerator df is k − 1 = 3 for four groups, so F(3, 76) is already right.
✗d. The arithmetic is right and the claim is wrong — that is the whole point of the question.
🏷️ When a sentence pairs a correct statistic with a confident claim, suspect the claim. “All of them differ” and “X is best” are the two that fail most often.
